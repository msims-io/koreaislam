These days you often see "halal restaurant" and "halal certified" signs in Korea too. It's a change that came with more Muslim tourists, and with it came various rumours. Let's look at them one by one.

## Halal and haram

- **Halal**: "permitted"
- **Haram**: "forbidden"

The words apply to every part of daily life, not only food, but in Korea they're mostly used about food.

## What don't Muslims eat?

- **Pork** and anything made from pigs (gelatine, lard and so on)
- **Alcohol** and anything intoxicating
- Blood, and meat not slaughtered properly in God's name

Vegetables, fruit, grains and fish are mostly halal. So Korean Muslims happily eat Korean food like kimchi, bibimbap and seafood pancakes. They do always check the ingredients on ramen seasoning, snacks and jellies for pork-derived ingredients.

## Why eat this way?

For Muslims, halal isn't only about health or hygiene; it is a practice of faith: **doing even eating in line with God's will**. That's why they call on the name of God, who gives life, when taking meat, and keep to methods that cause the animal less pain. There's something in common with the Buddhist precepts on killing and drinking, and the spirit of temple food.

## The rumour that "halal certification fees fund terrorism"

This story spread online in Korea from around 2015, but no investigation or official data supporting it has been found. Halal certification checks whether a product meets the standard, and the fee pays for the certifying body's operations, like any other quality certification. In Korea, bodies such as the Korea Muslim Federation (KMF) carry out halal certification.

More halal-certified products don't force anything on non-Muslims, either. Halal food is simply food that Muslims can eat too.
